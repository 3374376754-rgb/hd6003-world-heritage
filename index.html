01 / The question

A list is never just a list.

Every catalogue makes choices: what to include, how to classify it, and when it enters. This study asks three descriptive questions about those choices, using only the fields in the 2019 UNESCO World Heritage List.

Why not a Pythagorean Tree?

A Pythagorean Tree visualises a trained decision tree. It needs a target variable and a model. That would turn the project into prediction or model interpretation. The task here is descriptive analytics: describe the structure of the list, not predict a new site. The three chosen widgets keep the scope honest and make the comparison between regions and categories readable.

04 / The sieve

Where the list is dense.

The sieve compares two categorical variables: category and region. The area of each cell follows the count expected under independence. The colour shows the direction and strength of the departure from that expectation.

Read the largest cells first. Then look for the strongest colours: those are the combinations that appear more, or less, often than the marginals would predict.

Association is not causation. The sieve does not explain why a region has more Cultural sites; it only shows that the pattern is there. Small cells are unstable and should not carry a large argument.

Cell area follows the expected count under independence; colour is the standardised Pearson residual. Hover or focus a cell for observed and expected counts. Three multi-region records are omitted.

05 / The mosaic

What is inside each region.

The mosaic changes the question from association to composition. Each column is a region; the height of each segment is the share of a category. The colour shows the category mix, and the controls let you filter the same columns by danger status.

Mixed is a small category, so its segments are thin and unstable. Danger is an administrative status, not a measure of heritage value. Use the mosaic to compare shapes, not to rank regions.

06 / The violin

When sites enter the list.

The violin shows the full distribution of inscription years, not just an average. Width is density; the centre line is the median; the tails are the earliest and latest inscriptions.

Compare the median and the spread. A group that enters the list later has a shifted median. A group with a long tail has a few very early or very late entries.

These are inscription years, not the age of the sites. A site can be ancient and inscribed recently. The data stops in 2019.

07 / What the three views add up to

A cultural list with a regional tilt.

Descriptive patterns, not a ranking of heritage value. The numbers below are counts of records in the 2019 snapshot.

77.5%

Cultural gravity

869 of 1,121 records are Cultural. Natural accounts for 213 (19.0%), Mixed for 39 (3.5%).

47.1%

A regional tilt

Europe and North America hold 528 records. Asia and the Pacific holds 266; Latin America and the Caribbean 142; Africa 96; Arab States 86.

2.1×

Natural sites in Africa

38 Natural sites appear where about 18 would be expected under independence. In Europe and North America, Natural sites are under-represented: 65 vs about 100 expected.

The list is not random. But a pattern in the catalogue is not an explanation of why the catalogue looks that way.

One more signal: 21 of the 53 records marked “in danger” are Cultural sites in Arab States. The mosaic filter makes that concentration visible.

08 / What this is not

A map, not the territory.

The value of descriptive analytics is that it gives us a map before we start explaining. It also tells us where the map is thin.

01

A 2019 snapshot

The data ends in 2019. It is not the current list, and it cannot tell us about inscriptions after that year.

02

Administrative categories

Region and category are list classifications, not cultures. The page describes records, not the value of heritage.

03

Small cells, modest claims

Mixed has 39 records; some region–category cells are tiny. Small cells are unstable and do not support causal claims.
